A Simple Budget Template for Beginners (and How to Fill It In)

Updated 19 October 2026 · 7 min read

If you've never made a budget before, most advice makes it sound harder than it is. You don't need an app, a finance degree or a perfect record of last year's spending. You need one page, about an hour, and a willingness to be honest with yourself about where the money goes.

This guide gives you a simple budget template you can copy for free, onto paper or into any spreadsheet, and walks you through filling it in line by line. It works in any currency and any country, whether you're paid weekly, fortnightly or monthly.

What a budget actually is

A budget is just a plan for your money, made before the month starts. It answers three questions:

  1. How much money is coming in?
  2. Where does it need to go?
  3. What's left, and what do you want to do with it?

That's it. Tracking your spending afterwards is useful, but it's a separate job. The plan comes first, because a plan lets you make decisions calmly instead of discovering at the end of the month that the money has gone.

Four sections is plenty to start with. You can split categories further later, once you know where the detail would actually help.

Step 1: Write down your income

Start with what actually lands in your bank account, after tax and any other deductions your employer makes. Use your payslips or bank statements rather than your salary on paper.

If you're paid weekly or fortnightly, there are two simple options:

  • Budget per pay period. Make one template for each pay day and assign that pay to the bills and spending due before the next one.
  • Budget per month using a safe figure. Monthly pay periods don't line up neatly with weekly pay, so some months contain an extra pay day. Budget the month using the smaller number of pay days, and treat the occasional extra pay as a bonus for savings or debt.

If your income changes a lot from month to month, the method in our guide on how to budget on an irregular income will serve you better than a standard monthly budget.

Step 2: List your fixed bills

These are the costs that are roughly the same every month and hard to change quickly: housing, utilities, phone, insurance, loan minimums and any subscriptions you're keeping. Go through your last two or three bank statements and write down every regular payment you find.

Beginners often miss bills that come out quarterly or yearly, such as car registration, insurance renewals, annual software or memberships. Write those down too. You'll deal with them in Step 4.

Step 3: Estimate everyday spending

Groceries, transport, toiletries, coffee, takeaways, hobbies: these change from week to week, which is why they need a planned limit.

If you don't know what you spend, don't guess low. Look at the last month of bank and card transactions and add up each category. It's often a surprise, and it's much better to be surprised now than at the end of the month. If last month was unusual, use the average of two or three months.

A tip that helps beginners a lot: give "fun" its own line. A budget with no room for enjoyment tends to be abandoned within weeks. A small, deliberate amount you can spend guilt-free makes the rest of the plan easier to stick to.

Step 4: Plan your savings and goals

This section is where a budget starts to change your life rather than just describe it. Three lines are enough to start:

  • Emergency fund. A cash buffer for surprises like a car repair or a vet bill. Even a small amount each pay builds up. Many people aim eventually for a few months of essential costs, but a first target of one month's bills is a good start.
  • Sinking funds. Take each yearly or irregular bill from Step 2, divide it by the months until it's due, and save that amount every month. Our guide to sinking funds explains this in detail.
  • Extra debt payments. Anything above the minimum on your debts. If you have several debts, see debt snowball vs avalanche for how to choose which to pay first.

Step 5: Do the subtraction

Add up everything in the "Planned" column for bills, everyday spending and savings. Subtract that from your total income.

  • If there's money left over, decide where it goes now: savings, debt, or a goal. Unassigned money has a habit of disappearing.
  • If the answer is negative, your plan spends more than you earn. Go back through the everyday spending and savings lines first, since fixed bills are harder to change quickly. If it still doesn't balance, look at the bigger fixed costs over the coming months, and check whether you're getting all the income or support you're entitled to.

Some people like to keep adjusting until the "left over" line is exactly zero, so every unit of currency has a job. That's called zero-based budgeting, and we've written a step-by-step guide to zero-based budgeting if you'd like to try it.

A quick rule of thumb: 50/30/20

If you're staring at a blank page and have no idea what's reasonable, the 50/30/20 rule is a popular starting point. Popularised by Elizabeth Warren and Amelia Warren Tyagi in their book All Your Worth, it suggests roughly:

  • 50% of after-tax income on needs
  • 30% on wants
  • 20% on savings and extra debt payments

Treat it as a rough guide, not a rule you've failed if you miss. In many cities, housing alone takes more than half of a typical income, and on a tight income the "wants" share may need to be much smaller. The point is the shape: needs first, something for savings, and a limit on everything else.

Step 6: Track the "Actual" column

During the month, fill in the "Actual" column. You don't need to log every coffee in real time. A good beginner habit is a weekly ten-minute check: open your banking app, add up what you've spent in each category, and write it down.

At the end of the month, compare Planned with Actual. Where did you overspend? Where did you have money to spare? Use that to set more realistic numbers for next month. Most people need three months before their budget feels accurate. That's normal, not a sign that budgeting doesn't work for you.

A simple example

Imagine someone whose take-home pay is 3,000 a month in their local currency.

Section Planned
Fixed bills (rent, utilities, phone, insurance, loan minimum) 1,650
Everyday spending (groceries, transport, personal, fun) 850
Savings and goals (emergency fund, sinking funds, extra debt) 500
Total planned 3,000
Left over 0

Every unit has a job, and the plan includes a small amount for fun and a meaningful amount for savings. These numbers are only an illustration. Yours will look different, and that's fine.

Beginner mistakes to avoid

  • Making it too detailed. Thirty categories in your first month is a recipe for giving up. Start with the template above.
  • Forgetting yearly bills. These are the usual reason a "good" budget suddenly fails.
  • Budgeting with money you haven't received yet. Plan with what's certain, not what you hope for.
  • Quitting after one bad month. Overspending in month one is information, not failure. Adjust and carry on.
  • Not having a buffer. Without an emergency fund, every surprise becomes a crisis or new debt.

Paper or spreadsheet?

Both work. Paper is quick, satisfying and easy to keep on the fridge. A spreadsheet does the adding up for you. The best tool is the one you'll actually open every week.

The short version

  1. Write down your take-home income.
  2. List every fixed bill, including yearly ones.
  3. Set realistic limits for everyday spending, including fun.
  4. Plan savings: an emergency fund, sinking funds and extra debt payments.
  5. Subtract, and give any leftover money a job.
  6. Check in weekly and adjust monthly.

Your first budget doesn't need to be perfect. It just needs to exist. Each month you'll get a little more accurate, and a little more in control.
