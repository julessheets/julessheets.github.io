How to Write a Resume With No Experience (With Examples)

Updated 19 October 2026 · 7 min read

Writing your first resume can feel like a trap. Employers want experience, but you need a job to get experience. The good news is that "no experience" almost never means nothing to say. It usually means you haven't yet translated what you've done into the language employers look for.

This guide shows you how to write a resume (often called a CV outside North America) when you're a student, a school leaver, a recent graduate, changing careers or returning to work after a long break. It covers what to include, what to leave out, and how to turn everyday activities into convincing evidence of your skills.

What employers look for in a first resume

When someone hires for an entry-level role, they generally aren't expecting years of experience. They're trying to answer a few simpler questions:

  • Will this person turn up reliably and on time?
  • Can they learn quickly and follow instructions?
  • Can they communicate and work with other people?
  • Do they actually want this job?

Your resume's job is to give clear, specific evidence for those answers. School, volunteering, hobbies, projects and family responsibilities can all provide it.

Choose a simple, readable layout

Many employers, especially larger ones, use applicant tracking systems (ATS) to collect and sort applications. These systems read the text of your resume, and fancy designs can confuse them. Even when a human reads your resume, a clear layout is easier to scan quickly.

A safe layout for a first resume:

  • one page (that's plenty when you're starting out)
  • a single column, top to bottom
  • standard headings such as Profile, Education, Experience and Skills
  • a common font at a readable size
  • no tables, text boxes, photos or graphics carrying important information
  • saved as PDF when sending, unless the employer asks for a Word file

Whether to include a photo, your date of birth or other personal details varies a lot between countries. In many English-speaking countries it's common to leave them out. Follow the norms where you're applying.

What to include, section by section

1. Contact details

Your name, phone number, a professional email address and your town or city. A full street address usually isn't needed. If you have a relevant online portfolio or professional profile, add the link.

2. Profile (two or three lines)

A short summary at the top that says who you are, what you can offer and what you're looking for. Keep it specific to the job.

Reliable, friendly school leaver with two years of weekend volunteering at a community food bank. Comfortable handling cash, working in a team and talking to the public. Looking for a part-time retail role where I can grow into a supervisor position.

Avoid vague phrases like "hard-working team player with excellent communication skills" on their own. Show those things in the sections below instead.

3. Education

With little work experience, education often goes near the top. Include:

  • the name of your school, college or university
  • the qualification and dates (or expected completion date)
  • relevant subjects, projects or achievements
  • grades if they're strong or the employer asks for them

If you're a recent graduate, a few lines on a major project or dissertation can show initiative, research and writing skills.

4. Experience (yes, you probably have some)

"Experience" doesn't have to mean paid employment. It can include:

  • volunteering: charities, community groups, events, sports clubs
  • casual or informal work: babysitting, pet sitting, tutoring, lawn mowing, helping in a family business
  • school or university roles: class representative, club organiser, team captain, peer mentor
  • projects: building a website, running a social media account for a club, organising a fundraiser, making and selling things
  • caring responsibilities: looking after a family member involves organisation, reliability and problem-solving

Label the section "Experience" rather than "Work experience" so it can include all of these. For each item, add the role, the organisation and the dates, then two to four bullet points.

5. Skills

List practical skills that match the job ad: software you can use, languages you speak, a driving licence if it's relevant, first aid certificates, cash handling, customer service, social media, typing speed. Be honest. You may be asked about any of them in an interview.

6. Optional extras

Short sections for certifications (online courses count), awards or interests can help, as long as they're relevant or genuinely say something about you. "Reading and films" doesn't add much. "Captain of the under-18 football team for two seasons" does.

How to write strong bullet points

This is where a resume with no experience becomes convincing. A weak bullet describes a duty. A strong bullet shows an action and, where possible, a result.

A simple formula: action verb + what you did + result or scale.

Instead of… Try…
Helped at the school fair Ran a cake stall at the school fair, handling cash and serving a steady queue of customers
Responsible for social media Posted weekly updates for my sports club's page and grew it from a handful of followers to over 300
Volunteered at a food bank Sorted and packed food parcels with a team of six volunteers every Saturday for two years
Did babysitting Looked after two children aged 4 and 7 after school three days a week, including school pick-ups and homework help

Use numbers where you honestly can: how long, how many, how often. Specific details are more believable than adjectives. Only use figures you know are true.

Useful action verbs include: organised, led, built, created, served, sorted, trained, helped, managed, planned, improved, wrote, handled, solved, presented.

Tailor it for every job

Sending the same resume everywhere is one of the most common reasons for silence. Before each application:

  1. Read the job ad and highlight the skills and qualities it mentions.
  2. Make sure your profile mentions the most important ones.
  3. Reorder your bullets so the most relevant examples come first.
  4. Use the same key words the ad uses, where they're true for you. This helps both human readers and ATS software.

Tailoring doesn't mean rewriting everything. It usually means changing your profile and adjusting a few bullets, which takes about ten minutes once you have a good base resume.

An example layout

Here's how a one-page first resume might be structured:

  1. Name and contact details
  2. Profile: three lines tailored to the job
  3. Education: school or course, dates, relevant subjects or project
  4. Experience: volunteering role with three bullets; casual babysitting with two bullets; school club role with two bullets
  5. Skills: six to eight practical skills from the job ad
  6. Certifications: first aid, an online course

That's a full, credible page, without a single day of formal employment.

Common mistakes

  • Spelling and grammar errors. Read it aloud, use a spell-checker, and ask someone else to check it.
  • An unprofessional email address. Make a simple one with your name.
  • Too long. One page is plenty at this stage.
  • Overdesigned templates. Columns, icons and skill bars can look attractive but may not be read correctly by ATS software.
  • Exaggerating. Employers check, and interviews quickly reveal it. Confidence about real experience is more convincing.
  • No cover letter when one is asked for. A short, tailored letter explaining why you want this role can set you apart when experience is thin.

Writing a short cover letter

When you don't have much experience, a cover letter lets you explain your motivation. Keep it to three short paragraphs:

  1. Why this job and this employer. Be specific.
  2. What you can offer, with one or two concrete examples from your resume.
  3. A polite close saying you'd welcome an interview, and when you're available.

The short version

  1. Keep it to one page, single column, with standard headings.
  2. Lead with a short, tailored profile.
  3. Put education near the top.
  4. Count volunteering, casual work, projects and responsibilities as experience.
  5. Write bullets as action + task + result, with honest numbers.
  6. Tailor your profile and bullets for every job.
  7. Proofread, save as PDF and add a short cover letter.

Everyone's first resume starts with "no experience". The ones that work simply show what that person has already done.
