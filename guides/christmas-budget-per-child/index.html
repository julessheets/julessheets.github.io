How Much to Spend on Christmas per Child (and How to Set a Christmas Budget)

Updated 19 October 2026 · 6 min read

"How much should I spend on each child for Christmas?" There's no correct number. The right amount is the one you can pay for without borrowing and without a January money hangover, and that depends on your income, how many children you're buying for, and what else the season costs you.

What helps is a method. This guide shows what other parents say they spend, so you have some context, then walks you through setting a per-child budget from the money you really have. It takes about 20 minutes.

What other parents say they spend

Surveys give a rough sense of the range. Treat them as context, not a target.

  • UK: In YouGov's Big Survey on Christmas (November 2025), 45% of parents buying for their children said they spend up to £100 per child and 49% spend more. One in five (20%) spend more than £200 per child, and 6% spend less than £30. Among parents of children under 18, 61% spend more than £100 per child, including 27% who spend more than £200. Spending rises with household income (YouGov, 2025).
  • US: The National Retail Federation's 2025 holiday survey of 8,247 adults found people planned to spend $890.49 each on the holidays on average, of which $627.93 was for gifts for family and friends. That figure covers everyone on the list, not just children. The survey's research partner noted that families with children expected to raise their gift budgets by more than $30 on average (NRF, 2025).

Two things stand out. First, the spread is huge: some parents spend under £30 per child and others over £200. Second, spending tracks income. So "normal" covers a wide range, and the only number that matters is the one that fits your budget.

Step 1: Work out your total Christmas money

Start with what you can spend, not with the wish lists. Add up:

  1. Money already set aside for Christmas (a savings pot, a Christmas club, a sinking fund).
  2. What you can save between now and Christmas. Take the amount you can spare each week or each pay day and multiply by the number left before you shop. (From mid-October there are roughly nine weeks to go.)
  3. Nothing you'd have to borrow. If a card or buy-now-pay-later plan would be paying for it, leave it out. That money is next year's income.

The result is your total Christmas budget.

Step 2: Take out everything that isn't a gift

Gifts are only part of the season. Before you split anything between children, set aside money for:

  • Food and drink, including hosting and any parties
  • Travel to see family
  • Decorations, wrapping paper, tape and gift tags
  • Cards and postage
  • Outings, school events, teacher gifts, charity

What's left is your gift pot.

Step 3: Give the adults and extras a set amount

Decide what you'll spend on partners, parents, siblings, friends and anyone else, and take it out of the gift pot. A fixed amount per person (or a family Secret Santa) makes this much easier.

What remains is the children's gift pot.

Step 4: Divide it per child, and include the stocking

Divide the children's pot by the number of children. That's your budget per child. Then decide how much of it goes on:

  • Main gifts
  • Stocking fillers, which are easy to overspend on because each item is small
  • Gifts from the "big day" itself if you do them, such as a Christmas Eve box or a book

Stocking fillers count towards the child's budget. Leaving them out is one of the most common ways a careful plan quietly runs over.

A worked example

Here's one fictional family's plan. The numbers are just an example, and the method works in any currency.

Amount
Already saved $400
Plus $50 a week for 9 weeks $450
Total Christmas budget $850
Food & hosting –$200
Travel –$100
Decorations, wrapping & cards –$50
Gift pot $500
4 adults at $25 each –$100
Teacher gifts –$20
Children's gift pot (2 children) $380
Budget per child $190
…of which stocking fillers $30
…of which main gifts $160

If $190 per child feels too low, the honest fixes are to save a little more each week, trim the non-gift costs, or spend less on the adults. Moving the money around doesn't create more of it, but it does show you exactly where the trade-off is.

Keeping it fair between siblings

Parents usually pick one of these approaches. Each works, as long as you're consistent:

  • Equal money: every child gets the same budget. Simple and easy to track, but a teenager's single gift can cost more than a toddler's whole pile.
  • Equal number of gifts: everyone opens the same number of presents, and the spend varies. Younger children often care more about how many parcels there are than how much they cost.
  • The four-gift idea: something they want, something they need, something to wear, something to read. It keeps things balanced and puts a natural limit on the list.

Whichever you choose, write down the budget for each child and track what you've spent against it. Fairness is much easier to keep when you can see the numbers.

Ways to bring the per-child cost down

  • Agree limits with relatives early. A family Secret Santa, a price cap or "kids only" gifting frees up a lot of the gift pot.
  • Start with the list, not the shops. Note what each child has asked for and what they genuinely need before you browse.
  • Buy second-hand or pre-loved for things like bikes, games and books, where it makes little difference to the child.
  • Give one experience (a day out, lessons, tickets) instead of several smaller things.
  • Set a stocking limit per child and stick to it.
  • Buy early, then stop. Once a child's budget is used, their list is closed.

Start next year's Christmas fund now

The calmest Christmases are the ones paid for in advance. Once you know roughly what this year cost, divide it by 52 (or 12) and put that amount aside every week (or month) from January. That's a sinking fund, and our guide to sinking funds shows how to set one up. The short version

  1. Add up what you have and what you can save before Christmas, without borrowing.
  2. Take out food, travel, decorations, cards and other costs.
  3. Set a fixed amount for the adults on your list.
  4. Divide what's left by the number of children, including stocking fillers.
  5. Pick a fairness rule for siblings and track every child's spend against their budget.
  6. In January, start saving for next Christmas.
